# First impression and reader clarity

Review what a busy reader can easily find. Do not present simulated recruiter thoughts, fixed reading times, or callback predictions as observed facts.

## First-impression check

For a rendered document, inspect page one's opening area and the start of the most relevant recent role. For text-only input, describe the opening text and mark visual placement unassessed. Quote a short relevant excerpt if useful; remove unnecessary contact details and label redaction. Do not call a reformatted code block a literal rendering of the page.

Answer:

- What target or transferable role identity is visible?
- What is the strongest specific evidence near the beginning?
- What relevant evidence is buried later?
- What remains unclear about responsibility, level, or direction?
- What single ordering or wording change would help most?

Use “A reader may miss...” or “The opening does not establish...” rather than “Recruiters will reject...” Two or three proof points can be useful, but their absence is not a fatal flaw. A summary is optional when the headline and experience already establish fit. A brief transition objective can help when it explains a deliberate move.

## Organization

Use a concise role identity grounded in the candidate's work or clearly stated target. Keep a target headline distinct from historical job titles. Group skills by relevant domain, bring strong bullets forward within each role, and compress older work when it adds little to the target. Preserve enough context to understand chronology and concurrent work.

Prioritize action, artifact, constraints, scope, and results over adjectives. Do not maximize numbers or keyword density at the cost of readability. Avoid duplicating the same accomplishment in summary, highlights, and experience unless the short summary adds useful navigation.

## Audience adjustments

Technical readers may need method, telemetry, architecture, troubleshooting, or validation detail. Leadership readers may need decision rights, tradeoffs, resources, and organizational outcomes. Recruiters may need clear product names and role alignment. A single truthful bullet can serve more than one audience; do not assume a recruiter lacks technical literacy.

Use consistent emphasis, spacing, contrast, and legible type. Judge the rendered result, not a fixed count of bold words. A public profile, personal website, or conference record can differentiate when relevant, but none is mandatory.
